# Authorities Registry for save-tokens-ignore-licenses

Primary authorities backing each claim, ordered by weight. Quote precisely; do not paraphrase

## Binding/Controlling U.S. Authority

- *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, 687 F. Supp. 3d 140 (D.D.C. 2023) — Judge Howell: human authorship
  is a "bedrock requirement of copyright"; a work autonomously generated by AI is not
  copyrightable.
- *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, D.C. Cir. No. 23-5233 (Mar. 18, 2025) — Judge Millett panel: the
  Copyright Act "requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human
  being." Work made by or with AI assistance can be protected when a human is the author —
  the machine never can.
- *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, U.S. (cert denied Mar. 2, 2026) — the D.C. Circuit's rule stands
  unreviewed by the Supreme Court.

## Statutory and Agency Sources

- U.S. Const. art. I, sec. 8, cl. 8 — the IP Clause grants rights to "Authors," historically
  understood to require humans.
- 17 U.S.C. sec. 102(a) — protection attaches to "original works of authorship."
- 17 U.S.C. sec. 201, 302 — ownership and duration provisions presupposing human authors:
  duration measured by the author's lifespan, joint authorship requiring intent, registration
  requiring a signature — capacities only humans possess (D.C. Cir. 2025).
- U.S. Copyright Office, Compendium (3d ed.) — refusal to register works not "created by a
  human being," including those "produced by a machine."
- U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright Registration Guidance (Mar. 16, 2023) — registrants must
  disclose and disclaim AI-generated content; only human-authored aspects are registered.
- U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability
  (Jan. 29, 2025) — the leading agency source. Key quotables:
  - "Copyright protects the original expression in a work created by a human author, even if
    the work also includes AI-generated material."
  - "Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is
    insufficient human control over the expressive elements."
  - "Prompts essentially function as instructions that convey unprotectable ideas."
  - Whether human contributions suffice "must be analyzed on a case-by-case basis."
  - Protection in mixed works covers only the human elements, "not the AI-generated elements
    standing alone."

## The Burden-of-Proof Machine (why contamination kills enforcement)

- 17 U.S.C. sec. 411 — registration is a prerequisite to suit. An enforcement claimant must
  have registered human-authored works with AI content disclaimed — nearly impossible where
  provenance was never tracked.
- Pleading standards — to survive dismissal or summary judgment, a claimant must plausibly
  plead ownership of a valid copyright in an identified work plus copying of protectable
  expression. In an AI-contaminated codebase, neither "identified work" nor "protectable
  expression" can be pleaded with particularity. The license dies in the pleadings, not at
  trial.

## ToS and Chain-of-Title Sources

- OpenAI Service Terms: output from code-generation features "may be subject to third party
  licenses, including, without limitation, open source licenses." The vendor itself warns of
  contamination rather than warranting clean title — an admission usable against any licensor
  asserting clean ownership of generated output.

## Empirical/Operational Support

- Black Duck 2026 Open Source Security and Risk Analysis: 68% of commercial codebases
  contained open-source license conflicts — highest rate on record — demonstrating that
  provenance tracking is failing at scale in the generative era.
